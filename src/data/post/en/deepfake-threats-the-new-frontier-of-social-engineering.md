AI-generated audio and video convincing enough to impersonate real executives are no longer theoretical — several documented fraud cases have used deepfake voice calls to authorize fraudulent wire transfers.

## Key points

- A well-publicized case involved a finance employee transferring millions after a video call with what appeared to be deepfaked senior executives.
- Voice cloning now requires only a short sample of someone's real speech, often available from public earnings calls or interviews.
- Verification protocols that don't rely solely on voice or video recognition — like a pre-agreed code phrase — are becoming standard for high-value transaction approval.

## The takeaway

As deepfake quality improves, verification processes built for a pre-AI threat model need to be revisited across every organization handling sensitive transactions.
